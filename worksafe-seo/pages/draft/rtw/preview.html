Gradual return

What does a gradual return to work look like?

A gradual return to work means you go back in steps instead of all at once. You might start with shorter shifts, fewer days or lighter tasks, then add more as your body copes. A return-to-work plan is a written plan you and your employer make together. It sets out what you can do, your tasks and your hours. Each version is based on what you can safely do now, and a new one is made as you can do more.

  1. To start

    Begin with what you can do safely

    Changed days or hours, some tasks taken out or reordered, and stretch breaks and rest periods. Your physio can keep working on your strength while you build up your shifts.

  2. As you can do more

    Add hours, days and tasks a little at a time

    As you can do more, a new version of the plan is made. If the planned duties are not working out, the plan can be changed.

  3. The goal

    Working toward your usual job and hours

    Your full duties and your normal shift, when you can safely do them. Your physio can work on the hardest parts of your job before that last step.

How long it takes depends on your injury, your job and how your body responds, so every plan looks a little different.

Modified duties

What are modified duties?

Modified duties are changes to your job so you can keep working, or get back to work, while your injury still limits what you can do. WorkSafeBC says returning to work might mean doing other tasks, working a different schedule or doing things a little differently. The work your employer offers must be safe, useful to the business, within what you can do now, and a fit for your skills.

  • What can change

    WorkSafeBC suggests employers think about changes like these:

    • Your usual duties, for a while
    • Your days or hours of work, for a while
    • Taking out some tasks, or doing them in a different order
    • Stretch breaks and rest periods

  • What it can look like

    Every job is different. A few examples:

    • A warehouse worker with a back strain scans and labels orders instead of lifting heavy boxes.
    • A care aide with a sore shoulder does tasks that do not involve lifting or moving people.
    • A cook with a wrist injury preps with lighter tools and leaves the heavy pots to others.
    • A driver with a neck strain takes shorter routes with more breaks.
    • An office worker with a sore back switches between sitting and standing through the day.

TipBring any modified duties offer to your physio visit. Your physio can help you see which tasks in it fit what you can safely do now.

Who decides what

Who decides when I go back to work?

Several people have a part, and each decides different things. Since January 1, 2024, BC law says you and your employer must work together, and with WorkSafeBC, on a safe and timely return to work. Your employer must find and offer suitable work. WorkSafeBC manages your claim and benefits. Your physio and doctor can help you see what you can safely do at work. WorkSafeBC does not require medical approval before you go back.

  • You

    • Contact your employer as soon as you reasonably can, and keep in touch, unless contact would likely harm or delay your recovery.
    • Ask your physio or doctor what you can do at work and at home, and what to avoid. Then share it with your employer.
    • Help find suitable work when asked, and do not turn suitable work down without a good reason.
    • Give WorkSafeBC the return-to-work information it asks for.

  • Your employer

    • Must contact you as soon as practicable and keep in touch with you and WorkSafeBC.
    • Must find and offer suitable work that is safe, useful, within your abilities and a fit for your skills.
    • May make a written return-to-work plan with you that sets out what you can do, your tasks and your hours.
    • If it regularly has 20 or more workers and you worked there for at least a year before the injury, it also has a duty to keep you employed.

  • WorkSafeBC

    • Your case manager is your main contact. They manage your claim and decide on benefits.
    • A WorkSafeBC team member may help with your return-to-work plan.
    • Decides whether you get more treatment when your physiotherapist asks for it.
    • Can decide if you and your employer cannot sort out a disagreement about cooperating.

  • Your physio and doctor

    • Help you see how what you can do links to safe, suitable tasks at work.
    • WorkSafeBC does not require their approval for your return. A written plan can help keep them up to date, if needed.
    • May be part of making your return-to-work plan.
    • Your physiotherapist can ask WorkSafeBC for more treatment if you need it, and WorkSafeBC decides.

How physio helps

How can physiotherapy help me prepare for my job?

Your physio can look at what your job asks of your body and set treatment goals around those tasks, adding weight, time or reps a little at a time as you cope.

Parts of your job your physio can work on in the clinic

  • Lifting and carrying

    Lifting from the floor, waist and shoulder height, carrying loads, and working toward the weight your job needs.

  • Standing, walking and climbing

    Standing for longer, stairs, kneeling and squatting, and the walking your job needs.

  • Reaching and overhead work

    Shoulder strength and stamina for reaching, pushing, pulling and working with your arms up.

  • Gripping and tools

    Grip strength and control for tools, keyboards, steering wheels and tasks you repeat all day.

  • Sitting, driving and screen work

    Neck and back stamina for long sitting, plus a routine of movement breaks that fits your shift.

  • Balance and footing

    Balance and confidence for the floors, ramps and steps you deal with at work.

Your physio may give you exercises to keep up at home between visits. If you need more treatment, your physiotherapist can ask WorkSafeBC for it. WorkSafeBC decides. How WCB physio works at each claim stage

Your first visit

What should I tell my physio about my job?

Tell your physio what a normal shift really asks of you. Name the heaviest thing you lift and how often, how long you stand, sit or drive, and how much you reach, bend, kneel or climb. Mention your tools and your shift length. Say if your employer has offered modified duties. Also tell us the injury happened at work. Real detail helps your physio plan treatment around your job.

Before your visit, note down Checklist

  • Your job title and your main tasks
  • The heaviest thing you lift or carry, and how often
  • How long you stand, sit, walk or drive in a shift
  • Any reaching overhead, bending, kneeling, climbing or ladders
  • Tools, machines or vehicles you use
  • Your shift length, breaks and any overtime
  • What your employer has offered, such as modified duties or a return-to-work plan
  • Your 8-digit claim number, if it has arrived

Your employer may give you a Functional Abilities Assessment form for your health care provider. WorkSafeBC does not require this form for a return-to-work plan.

WorkSafeBC asks you to talk with your health care provider about what you can do at work and at home, and what to avoid. Then share it with your employer and talk about changing your duties or schedule so you can keep working.

TipA photo or short video of a hard task, taken where it is safe and allowed, can help your physio see what you do.

Warning signs

What warning signs should I tell my physio about?

Some extra soreness is common when you add hours or tasks, and it often settles with rest by the next day. Tell your physio soon if pain keeps building from shift to shift, or comes with numbness, tingling or weakness. Speak up, too, if you are changing how you move just to get through the day. If the planned duties are not working out, the plan can be changed.

Tell your physio if you notice

  • Pain that is getting worse from week to week, not better
  • Pain that has not settled by the morning after a shift
  • New numbness, tingling or pins and needles
  • Weakness, or a joint that gives way
  • Pain that wakes you at night or is there even when you rest
  • New swelling, redness or heat around the injury
  • Limping or guarding to get through a shift
  • A task in your plan that is harder than expected
Get urgent care

Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department if you have sudden weakness in an arm or leg, chest pain or trouble breathing. Do the same if you have numbness in your groin or inner thighs, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels.

Who else to tell

Keep your employer in the loop about how the plan is going. Your WorkSafeBC case manager is your main contact for questions about your claim.

WorkSafeBC programs

Is WCB physio the same as a WorkSafeBC return-to-work program?

No. WorkSafeBC runs its own Customized Recovery and Return-to-Work Program through a network of providers that hold long-term contracts with WorkSafeBC. Return to Work Support Services is another WorkSafeBC service, based at the workplace. These are separate WorkSafeBC services. Synergy Rehab does not deliver either service. FAQ

WorkSafeBC return-to-work questions

Do I need my doctor or physio to sign off before I go back to work?

Not for WorkSafeBC. WorkSafeBC does not require medical approval for you to return to work. A written return-to-work plan can help keep your physio or doctor up to date, if needed. Your physio can still help you see which tasks fit what you can safely do now. If you are unsure about a task, bring it to your next visit.

Can I say no to modified duties?

Under the duty to cooperate, you must not turn down suitable work without a good reason. WorkSafeBC can reduce or suspend wage-loss benefits if it finds you did not cooperate and had no good reason. If a task in the offer seems beyond what you can do right now, say so. Talk it through with your employer and your physio, and call your WorkSafeBC case manager, who is your main contact for your claim.

Will my wage-loss benefits stop if I go back part time?

Not always. Wage-loss benefits can continue when you go back on fewer hours or different duties. WorkSafeBC takes what you earn into account and may pay part of the difference. Your case manager can tell you how this works on your claim.

Does my employer have to keep my job for me?

It may. If your employer regularly has 20 or more workers and you worked there for at least a year before your injury, it has a duty to keep you employed. That means offering your old job or a similar one if you are fit for it, or else the first suitable work that comes up. Under the duty to cooperate, your employer must also find and offer suitable work for you.

How long does a gradual return to work take?

How long it takes depends on your injury, your job and how your body responds. Each version of your return-to-work plan is based on what you can safely do at the time, and a new one is made as you can do more. Ask your physio how your progress looks at each visit.

Can I keep going to physio after I go back to work?

It depends on your claim. WorkSafeBC says that once it accepts your claim, your health care provider should bill it for care related to your claim, and you should not have to pay fees. If your claim is still pending, a clinic may ask you to pay for treatment, and you can ask for a refund if the claim is accepted later. Ask the clinic how it handles visits while your claim is pending. Treatment for a body area that is not part of your accepted claim can be billed to you. If you need more treatment, your physiotherapist can ask WorkSafeBC for it, and WorkSafeBC decides. You can book WCB physio visits online.

What is a return-to-work plan?

It is a written plan you and your employer make together. It sets out what you can do, your tasks and your hours. A WorkSafeBC team member, your health care provider or a union representative may also help with it. It changes as you are able to do more.

I have not reported my injury yet. What should I do first?

Tell your employer as soon as it is safe, and tell anyone who treats you that it happened at work. Then report it to WorkSafeBC online at any time, or by phone through Teleclaim at 1.888.967.5377 toll-free or 604.231.8888 in the Lower Mainland, Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. Pacific time. Within 60 days of your injury, a WorkSafeBC-contracted physiotherapist can do your first assessment while your claim is pending, without prior approval. The clinic bills WorkSafeBC directly for that assessment. Treatment visits paid by WorkSafeBC start once the claim is accepted. Before then, a clinic may ask you to pay for treatment, and you can ask for a refund if the claim is accepted later. Ask the clinic how it handles visits while your claim is pending. Care for a body area that is not part of your claim can be billed to you.
